Method and limitations

The main diagram on the Flows page is a model, not a measurement. There are no free, publicly available publications on daily oil flows by country / route, so this moel combines monthly official statistics, tanker tracking, and a few explicit assumptions. This page explains where each number comes from.

Key terms

  • mb/d: million barrels per day. Every flow in the diagram uses this unit.
  • Crude and condensate: the unrefined oil that goes into refineries. Finished fuels such as diesel and LPG are not included. Some published figures cover “all oil”, which includes those fuels, so this page says which is which.
  • Refinery runs: the crude a refinery processes. Consumers in the diagram are refineries, plus a little crude burned directly for power, not drivers or factories.
  • Stock draw and stock build: oil taken out of storage and oil put into it.
  • Oil on water: the industry’s term for oil on ships. It includes cargoes sailing to a buyer (“in transit”) and tankers sitting idle (“floating storage”). The diagram calls cargoes on their way cargoes at sea.
  • AIS: the radio signal ships broadcast with their position. A ship can switch it off, which is called running dark.

The IEA reports for March to July are in this project’s files. Those for August and September are paid, so the figures from them come from the IEA’s free summaries and from press coverage.

How the flows are built

Production is EIA’s crude production through June. From July it follows the October edition of the STEO, so those months are EIA’s estimates and may be revised.

Consumption is refinery runs plus crude burned directly, from JODI. The world total closes on the IEA’s global refinery runs. There are two exceptions:

  • China: JODI’s China figure equals imports plus output minus exports in every month, so it hides any stock change. China uses official runs instead.
  • Middle East: from March it follows the IEA’s regional runs, because Gulf refineries were hit far harder than country data shows. Figures from July are forecasts.

Who sells to whom starts from the Energy Institute’s 2025 trade table, split further with UN Comtrade. A standard method called iterative proportional fitting rescales that table until it matches each month’s production and consumption. It keeps the pre-war pattern as far as the totals allow.

Routes for Gulf oil use published volumes. Each month’s Hormuz figure comes from the IEA. Bypass volumes come from the IEA and Kpler, with the rest of each country’s exports crossing the strait.

Daily values are shaped from those monthly figures, and each flow keeps its monthly average exactly. Hormuz flows follow the 7-day PortWatch tanker count, with pre-war swings at half weight. Other flows move in a straight line between mid-month points, with a break at the start of the war.

Where stored oil comes from

Each month the world refines more or less crude than it produces, and the difference must come from or go to storage. That world total is IEA runs minus EIA production. The diagram splits it into three sources, each drawn on the route the oil physically takes.

  • Stock draws are tanks in the consuming country, such as a government reserve feeding a nearby refinery. No transit is involved, so they run through Domestic.
  • Cargoes at sea are tankers loaded earlier that reach a refinery now. When the strait closed, ships already past it kept sailing and delivered through March, with no new oil behind them. They arrive by sea, so they run through Other seaborne. IEA figures based on Kpler data measure this. The same node on the right side shows the reverse: oil loaded but not yet delivered, as in the June rush of tankers.
  • Gulf storage is oil in tanks and idle tankers inside the Gulf. It runs through Hormuz, for the reason below.

Why a stock band crosses the strait

Most stored oil does not cross the strait. Gulf storage does, because the tanks are on the wrong side of it. Once the strait closed, producers could not ship their oil, so they filled tanks and parked loaded tankers inside the Gulf. That is the Gulf producer to Domestic to Stock builds band in March. To reach a buyer, that oil still has to sail out, so when it left it crossed Hormuz. The IEA says June’s surge was driven partly by releasing those barrels.

The model does not assume this. It takes Gulf output, subtracts Gulf refinery use and bypass loadings, and compares what is left with the published Hormuz volume. A surplus stayed in storage, and a shortfall left storage. The IEA separately measures Gulf storage from satellites and tanker tracking, and the two agree closely through June (see the checks below).

  • Japan, South Korea, Europe: the same JODI calculation as the US.
  • Residual: whatever the items above leave unexplained. It goes to India, Other Asia and Rest of World, in proportion to their imports, as stock draws or builds. These are not measured stock changes for those places.

The residual combines tanks nobody reports with errors in the world total. It is largest in March, at about 5.6 mb/d. The world total alone accounts for about 2.5 mb/d of that: it shows a 4.4 mb/d draw, while the IEA observed a 1.9 mb/d crude draw. The IEA calls this kind of gap the “unaccounted-for balance”. Its own gap for all oil has topped 2 mb/d this year, and it expects such gaps to shrink as data improves.

Why the graphic stops on August 31

The data after August is not good enough to report daily flows with confidence:

  • Country data ends: JODI runs through August, so later months have no reported refinery use or stock changes for most countries.
  • Hormuz estimates conflict: for September, Kpler and Windward put crude through the strait at about 10 mb/d and the US Energy Secretary at 13 to 14 mb/d. An Iranian commander claims 3 to 4 mb/d on the US-supervised route, and PortWatch counts about 1 tanker a day.
  • The volumes do not balance: using Kpler’s figures, about 4 mb/d crossing the strait could not be explained by EIA’s production estimates, and Gulf tanks could not have supplied that much for long.

In short, the monthly regional picture is fairly solid through June: how much left the Gulf, by which route, and how much came from storage. Confidence drops from July. Individual buyer-seller bands and single days are illustrations, not measurements.

What stands out:

  • Gulf storage is the strongest test, because nothing forces it to match. The model is within 0.15 mb/d in March and 0.6 mb/d at worst.
  • World stock change matches in February and April but misses by 2.5 mb/d in March and 1.3 mb/d in January.
  • Yanbu and Fujairah should come in under the IEA in February and June, because the model counts crude and the IEA counts all oil.

The same comparison caught this project’s errors. An audit of the earlier version found that it:

  • overstated Gulf refinery use by about 1 mb/d, which forced a release from storage in July and August that the data does not support.
  • filled too little Gulf storage in March, at 0.5 mb/d against the IEA’s 3.0.

Known limitations

  • No public daily country-level production or consumption data exists. True daily trade flows need paid tanker tracking, such as Kpler or Vortexa.
  • PortWatch counts vessels, not barrels, and does not separate inbound from outbound.
  • Crude figures rest on a share of about 0.82 of all oil in several months, which is itself an estimate.
  • Iranian exports are opaque, so treat Iran as an estimate with wide uncertainty.
  • Apart from the cargoes-at-sea bands, flows reach a consumer the day they leave. Dips and spikes at consumers therefore arrive sooner than refineries felt them.
  • World refinery runs exclude crude burned directly, while country figures include it. The effect is small, about 0.5 mb/d, mostly Saudi power plants, and it falls on Rest of World.
